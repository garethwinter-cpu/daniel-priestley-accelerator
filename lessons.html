Part 1

Oversubscribed Principles

Lessons 1–10
Lesson 1 — Only Oversubscribed Businesses Make ProfitYour job as an entrepreneur is to architect demand and supply tension.Open lessonClose

You've been told profit comes from creating more value. Daniel dismantles that belief with the first lesson of economics — bananas. 100 bananas and 1,000 buyers means profit; 100 bananas and 10 buyers means loss, no matter how good you are. There are no 'profit gods': airlines run brutal operations for slim margins while Rolex earns 70% by orchestrating waiting lists. You'll shift from supply-side thinking to architecting demand-and-supply tension — starting by naming your official capacity: the number of clients you can truly delight.

Key frameworks
  • The banana slide — demand-and-supply tension sets the price
  • 'There's no profit god' — airlines vs Rolex margins
  • Official Capacity — the number of clients you can delight
  • Perfectionist → directionalist
Exercise
Do thisName your official capacity. Decide the number of clients you can serve this year where every single one ends up delighted — then say it out loud and put it in your marketing.
AI PROMPT
Based on my business model and my capacity, how many clients can I take on each year and deliver a full and remarkable experience?
“Your job as an entrepreneur is to architect demand and supply tension.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 2 — First Make Your Market, Then Make Your SalesOne of the most powerful things you can do is market for signals, not sales.Open lessonClose

Stop selling your ten spots — build a market of hundreds who want them. Daniel shows you how to market for signals, not sales, collecting roughly 30 signals of interest per unit of capacity. Through Esther Perel's breakthrough with ultra-high-net-worth couples and Glastonbury's 787,000 registrations for 136,000 tickets, you'll see why 90% of your value depends on who's looking — and how to describe the tension between your capacity and demand until you carry genuine 'with or without you' energy.

Key frameworks
  • Market for signals, not sales — the 30:1 signals-to-capacity rule
  • Esther Perel — 90% of your value depends on who's looking
  • ICP — the person with the power to pay the most
  • Glastonbury — register-only tickets, gone in 36 minutes
Exercise
Do thisZoom in on who you've previously worked with: which type of customer sees the most value in what you do — or has the power to pay the most for it?
AI PROMPTS
Based on what you know about me and my market, who is the ideal customer persona I would be best to target and at what price point? Consider that I want to target an ICP with available budget to spend (either discretionary income or an existing budget). I want them to easily justify their spending through savings, upside or experience. I want them to be a pleasure to work with and I can easily deliver the outcomes they want.
I want to run a marketing campaign to attract and engage my ideal customer persona. I want them to signal their interest in solving a problem or getting an outcome. They will either register for an event, complete an assessment, request a sample, join a discussion group, or some other free step. Suggest 10 specific campaigns that I could run to get people to signal their intention of working with me for free.
“One of the most powerful things you can do is market for signals, not sales.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 3 — It Takes 11 Interactions, 7 Hours, 4 Locations for People to Be Pre-SoldYou don't write a hit song, you tour a hit song. That's how a hit becomes a hit.Open lessonClose

People notice you for the first time when they see you for the eleventh time. In this lesson you'll internalise 11-7-4 — 11 interactions, 7 hours, 4 locations — the research-backed journey every buyer travels before they're pre-sold. Daniel shows you why you must tour your message like Bon Jovi toured Living on a Prayer, explain what you do simply, and build a content ecosystem the new semantic algorithms can deliver to exactly the right person. Prolific beats perfect.

Key frameworks
  • 11-7-4 — interactions, hours, locations
  • 'You don't write a hit song, you tour a hit song'
  • Semantic algorithms — every post finds your perfect person
  • Prolific beats perfect — Dolly Parton's 1,000 songs
Exercise
Do thisAudit your own 11-7-4 ecosystem with the content-audit prompt, then answer: what will you do in the next 30 days to improve it?
AI PROMPTS
Search online for quality content that features me. How many pieces of quality content can you easily find? How many hours can someone spend freely watching or reading my content to get to know me?
Based on what you know about my core offer, what are 10 beliefs that someone would have to have in order for them to enthusiastically buy from me?
“You don't write a hit song, you tour a hit song. That's how a hit becomes a hit.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 4 — Decommoditise Through Philosophy & ExperienceHe didn't talk about boats — he talked about reconnecting families.Open lessonClose

The moment you describe yourself with features and benefits, you become Googleable — and commodities race to the lowest price. Here you'll craft the philosophy that makes you incomparable, the way Simon Sinek owns 'start with why' and Daniel's client Darren sold boats by talking about reconnecting families. You'll write your own 'If you think it's about X, you've missed the point' statement, then learn how crafted experiences — theatre-venue events, quarterly dinner parties — put you in a category of one.

Key frameworks
  • Commoditised = easily compared
  • Decommoditise through philosophy — the bigger game you're playing
  • 'If you think it's about ___, you've missed the point'
  • Decommoditise through experience — events that can't be compared
Exercise
Do thisFill in and say aloud: 'If you think it's about ___, you've missed the point. It's really about ___.'
AI PROMPTS
I want to share the deeper philosophy that drives what I do professionally. I want to share a statement that makes people realise there's a bigger game that I'm playing — beyond the products and services that I sell. Based on what you know about me, what do you think this bigger game would be? What would this statement be?
I want to create an experience that leaves a remarkable impression for my clients and potential clients. They will feel compelled to share it on social media along with photos and videos. This experience will differentiate me from my competitors, putting me in a category of one in the eyes of my prospects. Give me ten ideas to explore.
“He didn't talk about boats — he talked about reconnecting families.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 5 — Buying Environments Causes BuyingWe don't buy stuff. We buy access to the movie.Open lessonClose

The same thing in a different environment sells at a different price and velocity. Daniel walks you through the masters — Apple's art-gallery stores staffed by 'geniuses', Rolex's security glass and locked doors — then down to your own Zoom background, where £150 of lighting decides your credibility in a tenth of a second. You'll learn the three intangibles that trigger a buying stampede: logic (the spreadsheet), emotion (the movie in your customer's head), and urgency — used only as a spice.

Key frameworks
  • Buying environments cause buying — Apple, Rolex, QVC
  • The £150 Zoom transformation
  • Logic + Emotion + Urgency — urgency is a spice
  • 'We don't buy stuff, we buy access to the movie'
Exercise
Do thisAudit your own buying environment: if you were Apple or Rolex, how would you transform it so it's obvious people should buy from you? Then check which needs more work — your logic, emotion or urgency.
AI PROMPTS
Help me build a calculator that shows return on investment for buying my product or service, assuming the buyer is my ideal customer persona. Help me build a logical case I can calculate on a spreadsheet.
I suspect my potential clients have a dream moment they imagine happening in the future that I'm able to get them closer to. It's like a movie they play in their mind. When I tell them I can help them achieve this movie, they get excited to buy from me. What's the fantasy they have in their head?
“We don't buy stuff. We buy access to the movie.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 6 — People Buy to Solve ProblemsYou need to meet them at the problem, not meet them at the solution.Open lessonClose

People live in the problem, not the solution — so that's where you meet them. Daniel shares how one rewritten headline ('Feeling crabby watching your investments move sideways?') earned him $90,000 by moving an ad from solution-aware to problem-aware. You'll learn the situation model every buyer constructs — current reality, desired reality, obstacles — and the three priming questions that make people ready to buy. Your homework: map it for your favourite client.

Key frameworks
  • Meet them at the problem, not the solution
  • The situation model — current reality, desired reality, obstacles, path of least resistance
  • Three priming questions + 'what have you tried that didn't work?'
  • Get a PhD in your customers' problems
Exercise
Do thisMap the situation model for your favourite client: their less-than-perfect current situation, their desired outcome, the obstacles, what they'd tried that didn't work — and how you became their path of least resistance.
AI PROMPT
Considering what I do professionally, what is the pain that I help people to solve? What is the obstacle that I remove? What is the prize that I deliver? Why do people see my offer as a path of least resistance? And what is the pain that people experience if they don't buy?
“You need to meet them at the problem, not meet them at the solution.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 7 — People Buy What They Want, Not What They NeedIf you need to buy the thing, you don't actually want the thing.Open lessonClose

Singapore — 80 km/h speed limits, cameras everywhere, million-dollar price tags — has more Ferraris per capita than almost anywhere on Earth. Why? People buy what they want, never what makes sense. Daniel hands you the truck-and-trailer model: values are an unstoppable truck you'll never derail, so hitch your offer to it. You'll confront the fitness-trainer paradox — the people who need you don't value what you sell — and practise linking what you do to freedom, security, adventure, family or status.

Key frameworks
  • The Singapore Ferrari paradox
  • The truck and trailer — link your offer to their values
  • The fitness-trainer paradox
  • Sell what they want, package in what they need
Exercise
Do thisExplain what you do to someone nearby, linked to the values your typical client holds — then run the values prompt and compare.
AI PROMPT
Considering what I do professionally, how would I explain it to someone whose highest value is freedom? Security? Adventure? Family? Fitness? Health? Wealth? Status?
“If you need to buy the thing, you don't actually want the thing.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 8 — People Buy What Others Want to Buy, Not What Others Want to SellEverything you do that says other people are buying is what people pay attention to.Open lessonClose

Everything that signals 'I want to sell to you' repels; everything that signals 'others are buying this' attracts. From the too-desperate voicemail, to the ice-cream queue you can't ignore, to Kate Middleton selling out a jacket in three days, Daniel shows you how demand displays do your selling. You'll reframe your copy from 'my program is perfect for CEOs' to 'many of my existing clients are CEOs' — planting the magic subtext: cool, calm, smart people already buy from you.

Key frameworks
  • Demand displays — queues, reviews, waiting lists
  • The copy reframe — 'many of my existing clients are…'
  • The magic subtext
  • Put your favourite clients in the spotlight
Exercise
Do thisPick your favourite client. Work out how to put them in the spotlight — on your YouTube, your Instagram, your website.
AI PROMPTS
Who are examples of high-profile successful people who buy and recommend services like what I offer?
If I could showcase any brand ambassador who would instantly make my offer more desirable, who would be my perfect brand ambassador?
“Everything you do that says other people are buying is what people pay attention to.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 9 — People Have No ImaginationA list? I can't trust someone who has a list. They could have put that in a wheel if they'd thought about it.Open lessonClose

Half your customer's brain is dedicated to visual processing — and none of it to imagination. Daniel proves it with property developers selling unbuilt towers from glossy CGI, the $5 million buyer who can't purchase an apartment until it's staged with a couch, and Porsche photoshopping cars onto driveways. You'll turn your intangible work into visual proof: a framework wheel, a before-and-after, a brochure worthy of a car dealership. When they can see the transformation, they sign up on the spot.

Key frameworks
  • 50% of the brain is visual processing
  • Property staging — nobody buys the unstaged apartment
  • The wheel vs the list — visualise your method
  • Before-and-after as instant proof
Exercise
Do thisDecide how you'll add more visuals in the next 30 days: a brochure, a landing page, a video, a before-and-after, and a diagram of your framework.
AI PROMPT
What would be the most compelling way to show what my business does visually? Then create a full brief for me to give to a designer to improve my brochures, landing pages and social media accounts to attract more high-quality ideal customers.
“A list? I can't trust someone who has a list. They could have put that in a wheel if they'd thought about it.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 10 — People Are Not ProactiveThe ball is always in your court. It is never in their court.Open lessonClose

Your customers will never call you back — the ball is always in your court. In this closing principle, you'll release the belief that sales is icky and see it as the high-end brands do: Apple trains its store teams daily; Rolex runs a bootcamp before staff may speak to a customer. Daniel gives you his 25-year engine — LAPS: Leads, Appointments, Presentations, Sales — plus clear calls to action and the nudge email that can pull six figures from your cold database.

Key frameworks
  • Homer Simpson customers — it's your job to prompt action
  • Clear calls to action everywhere
  • Sales is high-end — Apple and Rolex train relentlessly
  • LAPS — Leads, Appointments, Presentations, Sales
Exercise
Do thisAudit yourself: how many sales meetings per week do you have now, and how many do you want? Then run a nudge email campaign to the cold contacts already sitting in your database and DMs.
AI PROMPTS
Create a sales script for me to follow up warm leads that have expressed interest in working with me. Create a list of common objections people might have to working with me, and how I would handle them.
Write 10 examples of powerful calls to action that I can put at the bottom of my social media posts to get people to DM me, click a link or signal their interest in working with me.
Create a nudge email for me to follow up with people on my database who have gone cold. I want to elegantly re-engage their interest without seeming desperate.
“The ball is always in your court. It is never in their court.”— Daniel Priestley
Part 2

Oversubscribed Actions

Lessons 11–16
Lesson 11 — Campaign TeamGood people have jobs. They're already part of a team. Your job is to find rebels and misfits.Open lessonClose

All success is a team sport — and in this lesson you stop waiting for milestones and assemble the team that creates them. Daniel maps his four-role campaign team — Key Person of Influence, Head of Growth, Head of Delight, Head of Performance — now built around a central AI system, and shows you where your first recruits actually come from: rebels and misfits, not 'good people'. You leave knowing your role, your first outreach messages, and your Monday-morning meeting rhythm.

Key frameworks
  • The four roles — KPI, Head of Growth, Head of Delight, Head of Performance
  • AI at the centre of the org chart
  • 'Good people have jobs' — recruit rebels and misfits
  • The 3-to-6 rule — Monday meetings, Friday debriefs, 4–12 people
Exercise
Do thisIdentify who fills each of the four roles for your business (even part-time or trade), then send your first direct recruitment messages.
AI PROMPT
I'm launching a new business. Our marketing plan will follow Daniel Priestley's Oversubscribed methods — perfect repeatable week, quarterly spotlight and big message. I will need a team to help me execute. The key roles will be: Key Person of Influence (the reputation and face), Head of Growth (generating leads and sales), Head of Delight (making sure customers are cared for), and Head 'Swiss Army Knife' (getting general things done). Help me to recruit, onboard and align these team members.
“Good people have jobs. They're already part of a team. Your job is to find rebels and misfits.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 12 — Product & Service EcosystemProducts and services don't make money. Product and service ecosystems make money.Open lessonClose

One product will never make you money — and in this lesson you discover why. Daniel introduces the Ascending Transaction Model: a Gift that captures attention, a Product for Prospects that earns trust, a Core Offer that drives revenue, and a Product for Clients that delivers recurring profit. Through Gordon Ramsay's cookbooks-to-restaurants empire and Apple's iTunes-to-iPod ladder, you learn to float above your business and design an ecosystem where every product feeds the next.

Key frameworks
  • The Ascending Transaction Model — Gift → P4P → Core → P4C
  • DIY / done-with-you / done-for-you
  • Build order: P4P first, Core next, Gifts to scale, P4C last
  • The £117,000 add-on — a capped $39/month membership
Exercise
Do thisFloat above your business and design your four-part ecosystem: your Gift, your Product for Prospects, your Core Offer and your Product for Clients.
AI PROMPT
I'm productising my business. My core intellectual capital is about how to <XYZ> and my ideal customer is <ICP>. Help me to develop a product and service ecosystem using Daniel Priestley's Ascending Transaction Model. Gift: something of value I can easily give away for free. P4P: a low-risk first step to learn about and experience what I do. Core Offer: a full and remarkable solution all packaged up. P4C: an ongoing service, subscription, membership or upsell.
“Products and services don't make money. Product and service ecosystems make money.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 13 — Perfect Repeatable WeekYou sharpen in the market, not in your mind.Open lessonClose

Here Daniel reveals the dirty little secret about success: it's boring, repetitive — and it works. Through Bon Jovi's 803 concerts in six years and his own year-one week (one Sunday ad, one Wednesday workshop, repeated 43 times for $1.3 million), you learn to engineer a single week that manufactures demand, then crank the handle. You'll master his short-form / long-form / lead-form content engine and the LAPS pipeline, and work backwards from your client target to the exact leads-per-week your machine must generate.

Key frameworks
  • Bon Jovi — 803 concerts; crank the handle
  • Short form daily (Pain · Prize · Problem · News) · long form monthly · lead form quarterly
  • Four ways to signal — assessment converts 20–40% vs 2–4% for contact forms
  • Reverse-engineer: 30 clients ← 900 leads ← 23 leads a week
Exercise
Do thisReverse-engineer your own perfect repeatable week across 40 weeks. Start daily short-form posting and build an online self-assessment as your lead form.
AI PROMPTS
I am developing short-form videos to promote my business. Based on what you know about me, help me develop scripts that will engage my perfect clients. I want to hook attention, deliver something of value and then get people to comment a word in exchange for longer, more detailed content. Ask me any clarifying questions and then begin creating short-form video scripts.
I am developing long-form videos to promote my business. Based on what you know about me, help me develop scripts that will educate my perfect clients on my value proposition. I want to hold their attention, deliver real value and get them to then click a link to complete a self-assessment on my website. Ask me any clarifying questions and then begin creating long-form video scripts.
I am developing an online quiz / assessment that will highlight why my ideal customer would get value from working with me. Help me to develop this self-assessment tool so that when the right person takes it they feel confident in signing up to work with me.
Build me a dashboard to measure my weekly: Leads, Appointments, Presentations, Sales. The dashboard should allow me to track and measure my activity over the next 3 months.
“You sharpen in the market, not in your mind.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 14 — Quarterly SpotlightYou're not paying $10,000 for an hour on stage. You're paying for 12 months of brand association.Open lessonClose

Buzz doesn't replace revenue — it lifts it. In this lesson you plan four spotlight campaigns, one per quarter: a guest-speaker event, a sponsored stage, a dinner party, or a relaunch ('if Apple's allowed to do that, why aren't you?'). Daniel's masterstroke is booking a speaker six months out, so you're not buying an hour on stage — you're buying twelve months of brand association, trust transference, and a reason to talk to everyone.

Key frameworks
  • Spotlights create buzz; repeatable weeks make the sales
  • Trust transference — share stages with recognised names
  • The quarterly rhythm — announce, engage, deliver, repeat
  • Relaunch as new — like Apple's September iPhone
Exercise
Do thisSit down with your team and plan your four quarterly spotlight campaigns for the year. Consider booking one guest speaker six months out.
AI PROMPTS
I want to run a special quarterly marketing campaign designed to promote my business, reengage past prospects and attract attention from new buyers. I want it to be special and unique from my other marketing. Ask me questions and make suggestions for this campaign. Then create a detailed campaign marketing and logistics plan for me to execute.
I'm hosting a special event for my business in 6 months. I want a special guest speaker who will be a drawcard for my clients. This person should be successful but not a superstar. They probably have 10,000 to 100,000 social media followers, a book they've written and a powerful story/ideas that my clients will want to hear. Ideally they live close to <city> and they will agree to speak for less than <budget>. Suggest 10 options.
“You're not paying $10,000 for an hour on stage. You're paying for 12 months of brand association.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 15 — Annual Big MessageIf they haven't heard from you in 90 days, their brain starts to archive you.Open lessonClose

One powerful idea creates an entire year's worth of marketing. In this lesson you choose the big message you'll repeat all year — because if people haven't heard from you in 90 days, their brain archives you. Then Daniel hands you his podcast pyramid: start on tiny shows, become every host's best-performing guest, and climb — exactly how he reached the biggest shows in business. You'll form a PLC group, rehearse your three-minute opening, and tour your message everywhere.

Key frameworks
  • One big idea, repeated all year
  • The 90-day archive rule
  • Climb the podcast pyramid — be the best guest at every level
  • The PLC group — posts, likes and comments
Exercise
Do thisDefine your big message for the year. Then start climbing: DM small shows (500–5,000 subscribers), form a PLC group, and rehearse your three-minute opening hook.
AI PROMPTS
I want to be a guest on more podcasts and YouTube channels. I'm looking for small and niche platforms to start with — ideally 500–5,000 subscribers. Suggest 20 podcasts or YouTube channels I could go on that would be ideal for my brand.
I'm appearing as a guest on a podcast. I want to launch in with a powerful opening hook that captures the audience and makes them want to watch or listen to the whole episode. Craft an opening hook based on what you know about me.
“If they haven't heard from you in 90 days, their brain starts to archive you.”— Daniel Priestley
Lesson 16 — Celebrate & InnovateThe juice is not worth the squeeze. There is no ginormous pot of gold at the end of the rainbow — celebrate as you go.Open lessonClose

Your brain is a dopamine engine — starve it and it stops playing the game. Using Super Mario's coin-by-coin reward loops, Daniel shows you why entrepreneurs who defer every celebration burn out, and how celebrating small wins with your team keeps the whole machine running. Then comes the innovate half: exporting real campaign data into your AI to reveal your true ICP, sharpen your offer, and turn your top-performing posts into ads. Review, celebrate, level up.

Key frameworks
  • The Super Mario dopamine loop
  • Friday debriefs — celebrate, then 'what could we have done better?'
  • Export campaign data → AI segments your real ICP
  • Audit your last 30 posts — turn sleepers into ads
Exercise
Do thisInstitute weekly celebration and review: Friday debriefs, journal first-time wins. Then run the data loop — export your lead and assessment data into your AI, and audit your last 30 posts for ad candidates.
AI PROMPTS
I have collected data from potential customers who have engaged an online self-assessment called <name>. Analyse the data provided and then: share the customer segments represented; highlight the ICP I should be targeting; improve my marketing message to attract more of my ICPs; suggest ways to improve my offer and the correct pricing for this ICP; create a basic financial forecast and timeline if I follow up correctly with my current leads; share any other insights that might be valuable.
Create a detailed description or infographic about my ICP so I can show my sales team and they will completely understand our ideal customer and what makes them engage and buy from us.
“The juice is not worth the squeeze. There is no ginormous pot of gold at the end of the rainbow — celebrate as you go.”— Daniel Priestley
